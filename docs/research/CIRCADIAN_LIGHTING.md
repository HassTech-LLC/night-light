# Circadian Lighting Research for Night Light by HT

**Reviewed:** September 8, 2026  
**Population boundary:** The general recommendations below apply to healthy adults with a regular daytime-oriented life. They are not a treatment protocol for shift work, a circadian-rhythm disorder, insomnia, bipolar disorder, seasonal affective disorder, children, or other populations with different light sensitivity.

## Answer: sunset is the right primary automatic cue

When a user does not keep a dependable bedtime, local sunset is a better automatic anchor than asking the user to invent one. Human circadian timing evolved around the natural light-dark cycle. In a controlled field study, a week of natural light exposure while camping moved participants' circadian timing about two hours earlier; average melatonin onset occurred near sunset and offset occurred before waking shortly after sunrise.[1] Follow-up work found seasonal adaptation and meaningful earlier shifts even after a weekend in natural light.[2]

That supports a **sunset-led** product. It does not support simply switching the display orange at the exact minute of sunset. Natural dusk changes spectrum and intensity continuously, and modern users remain exposed to room lights, phones, TVs, and monitors after sunset.

The best product model is therefore:

1. Sunset starts the automatic dusk progression.
2. The transition is gradual so it is comfortable and does not appear to flip.
3. Locally learned sleep timing acts as a safety guardrail when the person's likely sleep is earlier than the solar-only curve would protect.
4. Sunrise and wake activity restore neutral output and encourage strong daytime light.

## Why bedtime still matters as an optional guardrail

Expert consensus recommends no more than 10 melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (m-EDI) lux at the eye beginning at least three hours before habitual bedtime, no more than 1 m-EDI lux during sleep, and at least 250 m-EDI lux during daytime.[3] That does not mean every user must enter a bedtime. It means the app should avoid letting a late summer sunset leave someone under bright display light inside their likely three-hour presleep window.

A user-provided bedtime can be optional. Adaptive mode can instead estimate a broad likely sleep window from local-only observations such as the last sustained computer activity, display-off, session lock, and first activity after waking. The estimate should remain transparent, editable, and deletable.

## What the filter must actually control

### Melanopic output, not orange appearance

Melanopsin-related retinal pathways are particularly sensitive to short-wavelength light. The most useful practical metric is m-EDI at the eye, not color temperature alone. A controlled display experiment in 72 adults manipulated melanopic output while holding visual color and luminance comparable. Lower melanopic conditions produced earlier melatonin onset, less suppression, and shorter sleep latency in the brightest comparison.[4]

Night Light by HT can reduce melanopic display output, but it cannot calculate an absolute biological dose from a Kelvin slider alone. Display panel spectra, hardware brightness, HDR, viewing distance, content, and ambient lighting all change the result.

### Brightness and spectrum must change together

An iPad Night Shift experiment found no significant suppression difference between warmer and cooler Night Shift conditions when brightness was not also reduced.[5] A useful Smart Mode must reduce both short-wavelength contribution and effective intensity.

### The whole room matters

In 104 adults, ordinary room light below 200 photopic lux delayed melatonin onset and shortened melatonin duration by about 92 minutes compared with dim light below 3 lux. Presleep room light reduced melatonin concentration by 71.4% among participants whose melatonin had begun before bed.[6] A perfect monitor curve cannot cancel overhead lighting or another device.

## Why the change should begin early and remain gradual

A 2025 phone-display study found that dynamic modes reaching low melanopic output only near bed were less protective when earlier exposure remained relatively high. The authors found cumulative exposure over the final two hours useful in their experiment and proposed 35 m-EDI lux-hours as an experimental threshold.[7] This is interesting supporting evidence, not a general medical standard.

A related dusk-simulation experiment compared gradual and abrupt two-hour reductions. Holding high melanopic output for an hour and then dropping it abruptly suppressed melatonin more than a matched medium condition, while the gradual condition performed comparably to the medium condition.[8] The work supports avoiding a late cliff, although its exact curve should not be treated as universally proven.

For Night Light by HT, gradual means a continuous eased progression updated every 15–30 seconds. Users should notice that the screen is warmer when comparing hours apart, but not perceive a specific step when it changed.

## Individual differences prevent a universal guarantee

In a controlled dose-response study, the light level associated with 50% melatonin suppression ranged from about 6 to 350 photopic lux across individuals.[9] A highly sensitive person responded to 10 lux about as strongly as a less-sensitive person responded to 400 lux. The app therefore needs a simple More Protection control and must not label a fixed percentage “melatonin safe.”

## Product conclusion

The defensible default is **Solar Sync with Adaptive Guardrails**:

- begin a subtle ramp before or around local sunset;
- progressively lower blue-rich output and intensity across dusk and the early night;
- learn a broad sleep window locally, without requiring a fixed bedtime;
- accelerate only when necessary to protect a likely presleep window;
- return to neutral around sunrise or confirmed waking;
- never stack with Windows Night Light;
- recommend dimmer room lighting and screen-off at the eventual sleep point.

Appropriate public language is “reduces evening melanopic display output” and “follows local sunset using published circadian-light research.” Claims that the app prevents melatonin suppression, treats insomnia, or guarantees better sleep require optical and clinical validation that the current project does not have.
